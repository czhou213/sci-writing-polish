# Language: Precision, Coherence, and Readability

## Edit meaning before surface style

Identify the sentence's subject, action, scientific claim, and relation to the paragraph's main message. Repair grammar and information order before considering vocabulary substitutions. Prioritize meaning, clarity, and flow; preserve effective wording and the author's voice.

## Information order and sentence rhythm

Use the beginning to orient the reader and a natural point of emphasis, often the ending, for important new information. Connect sentences through a recognizable concept rather than a repeated connector. Keep subjects and verbs near each other; move long qualifications when they obscure the action without changing their scope.

Split a sentence when it contains competing messages or hides the main result. Keep clauses together when separation would obscure a comparison, qualification, or causal boundary. Vary sentence length rather than enforcing a word quota. Parallel grammar helps readers compare aims, groups, procedures, and findings.

## Concision without loss

Remove empty announcements and redundant nominalizations when they add no scientific meaning:

| Before | After | Condition |
|---|---|---|
| It should be noted that the signal decreased after heating. | The signal decreased after heating. | The announcement adds no qualification. |
| We performed a comparison of the two algorithms. | We compared the two algorithms. | The action and comparison are unchanged. |
| In order to estimate the error, we used repeated measurements. | To estimate the error, we used repeated measurements. | The purpose remains explicit. |

Do not compress away eligibility criteria, dose schedules, analysis populations, exceptions, time points, or reproducibility details. `Few studies` and `studies` make different claims. `In the tested samples` can be an essential scope restriction.

## Precision and terminology

Use one stable term for each construct, endpoint, group, and method. Preserve distinctions such as accuracy versus precision, concentration versus dose, risk versus odds, and measured versus predicted values. Do not expand an abbreviation by guessing its meaning. Define abbreviations when needed for the requested excerpt; avoid repeatedly redefining them within a complete manuscript.

Replace an unclear pronoun with a supported noun: `this estimate`, `these measurements`. If two antecedents remain possible, query the author. Resolve misplaced modifiers, unclear comparisons, and ambiguous negation before polishing tone. For example, `only` must stay attached to what is actually restricted.

Prefer a familiar word when it expresses exactly the same meaning. A technical term is not inflated vocabulary merely because it is uncommon. Do not treat vocabulary tables as lists of interchangeable synonyms: `corroborate`, `confirm`, and `validate` have different evidential meanings, and `modality` can denote a specific type of measurement or treatment.

## Voice, verbs, and tense

Choose active voice when the actor matters and passive voice when the material, process, or measured entity should stay in focus. Do not invent an actor to eliminate a passive. A direct verb can reduce abstraction, but a stronger-sounding claim is not a stronger verb: `suggests` must not become `proves` for style.

Keep tense tied to function: completed procedures and observations generally use past tense; established knowledge, figure contents, and current interpretations may use present tense. Follow field conventions and meaning rather than applying a mechanical section-wide tense conversion. Preserve tense distinctions that signal an ongoing process or a prior event.

### Direct verbs and familiar wording

Use these as context-dependent options, not mandatory substitutions. Preserve the actor, action, technical meaning, and strength of the original claim.

| Wordy expression | More direct option | Meaning check |
|---|---|---|
| conduct an evaluation of | evaluate | Keep the object and evaluation criteria. |
| perform an analysis of | analyze | Retain any named analytical method. |
| make a comparison between | compare | Keep both groups and the comparison basis. |
| carry out measurements of | measure | Retain the instrument, unit, and timing. |
| provide an explanation for | explain | Do not imply a demonstrated mechanism if only a possible explanation is supported. |
| give an indication of | indicate; suggest | Choose according to what the evidence supports. |
| make use of; utilise | use | Preserve specialized meanings in quoted text or technical names. |
| commence; initiate | begin; start | Retain `initiate` when it names a specific process, such as transcription initiation. |
| ameliorate | improve; reduce | Specify what improves or decreases; retain the original technical meaning. |
| exacerbate | worsen; increase | Name the adverse condition or quantity; an increase is not inherently adverse. |
| facilitate | help; enable | `Enable` may imply making something possible; `help` may express a contributing role. |
| elucidate | clarify; explain | Clarifying a relationship does not necessarily establish its mechanism. |
| exhibit | show; display | Retain `exhibit` if it is natural and precise in context. |
| enhance | improve; increase | Distinguish a beneficial change from a numerical increase. |
| modality | method; approach | Keep `modality` for an established measurement, imaging, or treatment category. |
| paradigm | framework; model | Keep `paradigm` when its broader conceptual meaning is intended. |

For evidence-related verbs, choose by function rather than rhetorical force:

| Function | Candidate verbs or phrases | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Describe the work | examine, investigate, evaluate, compare, estimate | `Evaluate` implies assessment; `estimate` identifies a numerical inference. |
| Report observations | observe, measure, detect, identify, find | `Detect` depends on the method and detection conditions; do not turn failure to detect into absence. |
| Describe relationships | correlate with, be associated with, predict | Association, correlation, and prediction are distinct; specify whether prediction was actually evaluated. |
| Offer an interpretation | suggest, be consistent with, may reflect | Do not replace an observed result with speculation or add an unsupported explanation. |
| Describe supporting evidence | support, corroborate | State which claim receives support; agreement does not by itself establish validity. |
| Describe confirmation or validation | confirm, validate, demonstrate | Use only when the design and evidence justify the specific claim; these are not upgrades for `suggest`. |

### Voice and tense examples

The examples are fictional language exercises, not study facts to reuse.

| Purpose | Example | Editing decision |
|---|---|---|
| Emphasize an identified actor | We measured conductivity at room temperature. | Active voice makes the action and actor explicit. |
| Keep the measured entity in focus | Conductivity was measured at room temperature. | Passive voice is useful when the actor is irrelevant or already known. |
| Report a completed observation | Conductivity decreased after cooling. | Past tense reports the observed event without adding an explanation. |
| Guide readers to a figure | Figure 2 shows the measured conductivity. | Present tense describes what the figure contains. |
| State a current interpretation | This pattern may reflect changes in surface structure. | Present tense and calibrated uncertainty are appropriate only if that interpretation has a supplied basis. |

## Connections and scope

Choose a connector only after identifying the actual relation. Options in the same row differ in grammar and emphasis; do not cycle through them merely for variety.

| Relation | Candidate words and phrases | Usage and scope |
|---|---|---|
| Contrast | however, by contrast, in contrast, whereas, while | Make the opposing features explicit; `while` can also indicate time. |
| Concession | although, even though, despite, nevertheless, yet | Acknowledge a fact that qualifies an expectation; do not dismiss contrary evidence. |
| Similarity | similarly, likewise, consistent with | Identify what is similar; similar outcomes do not establish identical mechanisms. |
| Addition | also, in addition, furthermore, moreover | Add a relevant point; stronger emphasis is not always needed. |
| Supported inference or consequence | therefore, thus, consequently, as a result | Use only when the stated premises support the inference or consequence. |
| Reason or explanation | because, because of, owing to, due to | Assert an explanation only if supported; use `may reflect` for a genuinely uncertain interpretation. |
| Example | for example, for instance, such as | Mark an illustration rather than exhaustive evidence. |
| Clarification | specifically, that is, in other words | Clarify the same claim without expanding its scope or changing its meaning. |
| Sequence | first, next, subsequently, finally | Indicate actual procedural or narrative order; temporal order alone is not causality. |
| Restriction or qualification | in this cohort, under these conditions, within the tested range, except for | Preserve the relevant population, conditions, range, and exceptions. |

A repeated key noun or clear sentence order often gives a smoother transition than an added signpost. `Despite` and `because of` take a noun phrase or gerund; `although` and `because` introduce clauses. When joining independent clauses, write `A decreased; however, B remained stable` or use separate sentences rather than a comma splice. `Therefore` cannot supply missing evidence or establish causality.

## Observation, interpretation, and uncertainty

State directly what was measured or observed. Calibrate interpretations to the evidence without hedging every sentence. Keep numerical uncertainty, biological variability, model assumptions, and epistemic uncertainty distinct. `Significant` should not replace `important`, and a small P value does not describe effect magnitude.

Fictional language example:

> The estimated error varied across datasets. This variation persisted after normalization. Differences in dataset composition may explain the remaining variation.

The final sentence is appropriate only when supplied evidence supports that possible explanation; continuity alone does not justify adding it.

## Translation and final comparison

For non-English notes, reconstruct intended scientific relationships rather than translating word order literally. Use established English terminology; query a term with competing scientific meanings. Do not add a rationale or mechanism to make an incomplete note read like a finished argument.

Check every revision for changed actor, comparator, population, timing, negation, quantifier, unit, numerical precision, citation attachment, and claim strength. Read the finished paragraph for natural rhythm and recognizable focus. Avoid decorative transitions, promotional adjectives, and unnecessary rewrites of correct prose.
