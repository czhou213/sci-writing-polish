# Paragraph and Sentence Architecture

## How to use these patterns

Optimize **content and reasoning** before **language**. Identify the paragraph's job, its main message, each sentence's function, the supported evidence, and the connection to neighboring paragraphs. Then revise information order, topic/stress positions, signposting, voice, and wording.

The quoted functional chains below retain the course's English wording. The finer paragraph types and sentence frames are **editorial applications**, not the lecturer's verbatim templates. Arrows indicate functions, not mandatory sentence counts. A function may span several sentences; compatible functions may share one sentence. Do not force Methods or legends into a claim–evidence–interpretation pattern.

Use supplied facts, clarify supported relationships, and request missing evidence separately. Do not create a gap, mechanism, sample-size calculation, prespecified analysis, or validation to complete a pattern. Preserve P1/P2 identifiers for traceability. In standard mode, propose cross-paragraph moves; in deep mode, execute and explain them.

## Introduction

### Opening and importance

**Purpose:** Orient the intended reader and narrow attention to the research problem.

**Functions:** Topic/problem → supported importance → relevant current knowledge or practice → unresolved issue leading to the next paragraph.

**Language:** Start with a concrete subject rather than `With the rapid development of…`. Explain broad relevance without inventing global burden or generalizability. Use the sentence ending to foreground the issue the next sentence develops.

**Constructed frame:** `[Approach] is used to [purpose], but [specific limitation] remains unresolved.` Both assertions require evidence.

### Literature synthesis

**Course wording, p. 162:** “State claim about the topic” → “Support claim with evidence (published studies)” → “Further interpretation(s)” → “Emphasize why important to the reader.”

**Application:** Open with an evidence-supported synthesis claim; group studies by the claim or comparison they inform; explain their agreement, differences, or scope; show the connection to the research need. Keep citations attached to their claims. If only an author-by-author list is supplied, provide limited integration and identify the information needed for stronger synthesis.

**Language:** Prefer claim-led sentences to repetitive `X et al. found…` when justified. Different populations or conditions do not automatically establish a controversy. Do not infer a common mechanism merely from similar outcomes.

### Gap and rationale

**Functions:** What is known → precise unknown/limitation/controversy → why resolving it matters → question addressed here.

These gap categories follow course p. 124; the sentence sequence is an editorial application.

**Language:** A supported `It remains unclear whether…` is more precise than an unsupported `No study has ever…`. A small publication count is not, by itself, a consequential knowledge gap.

**Constructed frame:** `Existing studies have primarily examined [supported scope], leaving [specific question] unresolved.` Do not invent the contrast or unresolved question.

### Aim and scope

**Functions:** Primary aim aligned with the gap → necessary design, intervention/variable, outcome, and population → secondary aim or hypothesis only when actually supplied.

**Constructed frame:** `We aimed to determine whether [question] in [population] using [design].`

Use `prespecified` or `hypothesized` only if the material establishes that status. Whether the Introduction previews findings depends on the article and user preference. Do not end with an unsupported claim that practice has already changed.

## Methods

Organize around procedures and information needed to assess the study; a concluding interpretation is usually unnecessary.

### Design, setting, and time

Design → setting and study period → recruitment or data source → relevant governance details if supplied.

Distinguish prospective, retrospective, randomized, and observational designs precisely; tense does not establish design. Do not fill in ethics approval or registration.

### Participants or materials

Source population/material → selection process → eligibility criteria → actual sample-size determination or rationale.

Keep planned sample size distinct from analyzed sample size. Available database records do not imply a power calculation. Keep inclusion and exclusion scopes unambiguous.

### Intervention, exposure, or procedure

Technique/intervention and purpose → supported rationale if needed → actual sequence, doses, timing, conditions, and controls → implemented quality or bias controls.

Place the entity and action close together; divide long procedure lists at natural stages. Passive voice may keep the sample or technique in the topic position, following course p. 76. Never invent `we` as the actor or add unreported blinding or randomization.

### Outcomes and measurement

Outcome name/status → operational definition, threshold, scale, or unit → measurement time and method → relevant secondary or repeated measurements.

Do not promote a biomarker into clinical benefit or decide primary/secondary status as an editor. Align outcome terminology with Results.

**Constructed frame:** `[Outcome] was defined as [definition] and measured at [time] using [method].`

### Statistical analysis

Analysis population/question → method linked to its outcome/comparison → implemented adjustment and variables → actual missing-data or multiplicity procedures → actual sensitivity analyses → software and reporting details.

These are reporting positions, not a checklist of procedures to invent. Do not add multiple imputation, covariates, or `prespecified` because the paragraph seems incomplete. Each method needs a clear analytical target; avoid an unconnected list of test names. Preserve effect-measure names and distinguish adjusted from unadjusted estimates.

### Method development or modification

Technical problem → actual modification → supported rationale → supplied validation → applicable conditions.

If only the procedure is known, improve that description and ask for the missing rationale/validation. A plausible modification does not establish successful validation.

## Results

### Course paragraph chain

**Course wording, p. 312:** “Introduce figure” → “Discuss trends, patterns, and/or relationships” → “Summary/takeaway message.” The final message is qualified by “This is not an interpretation.”

Apply this chain to an evidence unit; do not make one paragraph per figure an absolute rule. Where the analysis needs orientation, introduce its purpose; where the result is straightforward, lead with the finding. Match integrated interpretation to the article's Results–Discussion convention rather than adding mechanisms to separate Results by default.

### Sample flow and analysis population

Screened/available samples → relevant exclusions and reasons → final analysis population and groups → necessary characteristics or missingness, linked to the table/flow diagram.

Flag inconsistent counts rather than harmonizing them. Do not reproduce every baseline cell or interpret nonsignificant baseline tests as proof of equivalence.

### Main finding

Question or key finding → comparator, direction, magnitude, and time → uncertainty and essential supporting detail → factual takeaway.

**Constructed frame:** `At [time], [outcome] was [estimate] in [group] compared with [estimate] in [comparator] ([effect measure and uncertainty]; Fig. [number]).`

Retain effect estimates and uncertainty together. Avoid replacing them with `significantly improved`. Do not supply missing values or force redundant figure introductions.

### Secondary, subgroup, or sensitivity analysis

Actual analysis status and purpose → main observation → relation to the primary analysis → necessary analytical boundary.

A significant result in one subgroup and a nonsignificant result in another do not alone establish a subgroup difference. Ask for the relevant comparison evidence when absent. Similar directions do not establish that all robustness concerns have been resolved.

### Null or unexpected finding

Question tested → actual estimate and uncertainty → limited evidence-based takeaway.

Keep a negative primary finding visible. Distinguish no detected difference from evidence of no difference. Do not invent inadequate power as the explanation in Results.

### Multiple panels or sequential experiments

Evidence-unit purpose → panels in an interpretable order → factual synthesis of that unit.

Split when more than one central question is being answered. A takeaway may close the entire unit rather than every paragraph or panel. Keep panel references accurate when moving sentences.

## Discussion

### Opening synthesis

Research problem and population if needed → most important finding → essential supporting achievements → what part of the original question has been answered.

**Course section framing, pp. 343–344:** “Reintroduce topic”; “Restate the research problem”; “Summarize key achievements.”

Do not repeat the abstract or all numerical details. State an inconclusive or negative primary answer accurately rather than replacing it with `promising`.

### Main finding, interpretation, and literature context

**Course wording, p. 345:** “Summarize key findings” → “Interpretation of the findings” → “Context with previous studies” → “Synthesize to highlight value.”

Keep this as the default functional chain; context and interpretation may exchange positions if the reasoning reads more clearly, as the course's worked example also illustrates. Make the final sentence explain what is now understood, rather than merely listing agreement with earlier studies.

**Language:** Use `may`, `suggest`, `likely`, or `possibly` for genuine interpretive uncertainty (p. 349). `Consistent with` does not establish a shared mechanism. Describe why studies differ only when the supplied evidence supports that explanation.

### Possible mechanism

Observation needing explanation → available mechanistic evidence → inference connecting it to this result → what is directly tested versus conjectured → targeted validation if supported.

Do not invent a plausible biological story. If no mechanistic basis is provided, identify what evidence would be needed rather than presenting speculation as polishing.

### Null or unexpected results

Accurate result → supported possible explanations → evidence for and uncertainty about each → testable remaining question → boundary of the main conclusion.

Interpretation should explain the result, not rescue a preferred conclusion. Exploratory subgroups may motivate hypotheses but do not erase a negative primary outcome.

### Strengths, when useful

Concrete design feature → identified problem it addresses → evidence and scope of that advantage.

Avoid unsupported `large`, `rigorous`, and `novel`; sample size alone does not imply freedom from bias. Do not add a separate strengths paragraph solely to fill a template.

### Limitations

Specific limitation → affected estimate, inference, or generalizability → actual mitigation and residual uncertainty → relevant improvement if warranted.

Organize multiple limitations by importance. Do not invent bias direction or force reassurance that conclusions remain valid. Narrow the conclusion when the limitation warrants it.

**Constructed frame:** `[Limitation] may limit [specific inference or applicability]. Although [actual mitigation], [remaining uncertainty].`

Only use this frame if the mitigation and remaining uncertainty are known.

### Implications and future directions

Supported advance → potential use for a defined audience/task → conditions before application or extrapolation → next validation addressing those conditions.

Distinguish a conceptual advance, research tool, and demonstrated clinical application. Avoid an empty `More research is needed`; specificity must come from the actual unresolved question, not an invented research program.

## Conclusion

Main answer → key evidence if needed → relevant implication and essential qualification.

Do not introduce new data, mechanisms, populations, or validated applications. A separate conclusion can be more than one sentence; the Discussion's last paragraph can serve this function.

## Abstract

Problem and need → aim and design/population/comparison → principal results and uncertainty → bounded answer and implications.

Use the course's four reader questions in [title-abstract.md](title-abstract.md). Implement the functions as a narrative paragraph or under supplied structured headings. Background should not displace primary results. Preserve negative findings and essential qualifications during compression.

## Figure and table legends

Descriptive title → panels and subjects/conditions → symbols, units, n, and error definitions → known statistical details and abbreviations.

This is a descriptive architecture, not a claim–literature–synthesis argument. Unknown SD/SEM/CI or replication definitions require author clarification; see [figures.md](figures.md).

## Worked revision: procedure order

Constructed language exercise, not a course quotation or real study.

**Original:**

> Samples were analysed in duplicate. A 10-mL blood sample was collected from each participant at baseline. We used assay A to measure marker B. The samples were stored at −80 °C until analysis.

**Original functions:** Replication → collection → assay and target → storage.

**Revised functions:** Collection → storage → target, assay, and replication.

**Revision:**

> A 10-mL blood sample was collected from each participant at baseline and stored at −80 °C until analysis. Marker B was measured in duplicate using assay A.

**Content and reasoning:** Restores procedural order and joins replication to measurement. Does not add serum separation, storage duration, or quality control.

**Language:** Reduces repeated nouns and uses passive voice to foreground the material and measured target.

## Missing-gap example

If the only supplied facts are that prior studies examined X and this study also examines X, improve the language but ask which population, outcome, condition, method, or uncertainty distinguishes the new question. Do not invent an unstudied long-term effect.

## Reporting substantive revisions

| Paragraph | Purpose/main message | Original issue | Revised functional sequence | Content and reasoning | Language |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Methods P2 | Sampling and assay procedure | Procedure order jumps | Collection → storage → measurement | Move collection first; join replication to assay | Reduce repetition; clarify the measured target |

Internally inspect every supplied paragraph. Report all paragraphs only when requested; otherwise focus on substantive changes. Specify whether sentence identifiers refer to the original or revision, and remove these labels from manuscript prose.
