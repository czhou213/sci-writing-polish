# Titles, Keywords, and Abstracts

## Titles

### Build the title from the study

Start from the supplied abstract, findings, or research notes. Extract three elements before drafting:

| Element | Question to answer | What to extract |
|---|---|---|
| Variable | What is being investigated? | Intervention, exposure, condition, strategy, method, or explanatory variable; retain the comparator when it defines the question. |
| Outcome | What is being measured or established? | The central endpoint, property, performance measure, or relationship; retain its direction only when supported. |
| Sample | Where does the evidence come from? | Population, animals, tissues, materials, datasets, systems, or theoretical domain, with essential scope restrictions. |

Also identify the actual study design and the principal supported message. The three elements are a construction aid, not a requirement to use three clauses or include every detail. Adapt them to the discipline; a theoretical title may foreground a property and its assumptions rather than an empirical sample.

Build a specific first draft that names the variable, central outcome, and relevant sample. Do not stop at `Evaluation of [intervention] in [population]`: that construction leaves readers guessing what was evaluated. If the outcome or main finding is missing from the supplied material, use a supported descriptive title where possible and ask for the missing information rather than inventing it.

### Choose the structure and claim strength

The two core structures below turn the extracted elements into a title. The additional variants change emphasis when the comparison or research contribution warrants it. These frames are adaptable editorial formulations, not quotations or journal-mandated formats.

| Structure | Construction frame | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Declarative, finding-led | `[Variable] [supported result verb] [outcome] in [sample]` | The principal finding can be stated accurately in the title. Choose the verb from the evidence, not the desired impact. |
| Non-declarative, outcome-led | `[Outcome] with/of [intervention] in [population]: [study design]` | Name what was assessed without announcing a direction; useful when design and comparison are central, including many clinical reports. |
| Non-declarative, relationship-led | `Association between [exposure] and [outcome] in [population]: [study design]` | The study estimates a relationship and a causal statement is not warranted. |
| Comparative | `[Outcome] with [A] versus [B] in [sample]: [study design, if useful]` | The comparator is necessary to understand the research question. |
| Method- or strategy-led | `[Method/strategy] for [specific task or outcome] in [domain]` | The contribution is an approach or capability; do not imply validated performance unless it was evaluated. |

A declarative title announces a result; a non-declarative title identifies the subject of investigation without announcing the result. Declarative does not necessarily mean causal: `[Exposure] is associated with [outcome] in [population]` is a declarative association claim. Conversely, a non-declarative phrase such as `Effects of X on Y` can still imply causality and needs appropriate support.

For a supported experimental effect, verbs such as `reduces`, `increases`, `slows`, or `extends` may state the direction precisely. For observational evidence, `is associated with` may be appropriate. Use `predicts` only when prediction was actually evaluated; use `improves` only for a supported beneficial change in a named outcome. An active verb is not permission to strengthen the science.

Choose between structures using the main message, evidence, audience, and supplied journal requirements. Do not require all laboratory studies to use declarative titles or all clinical studies to use non-declarative titles. A descriptive title is useful when the result is uncertain, but must not be selected to conceal an important negative primary result. A finding-led title must not turn a nonsignificant comparison into equivalence.

### Draft, compress, and select

1. Identify the principal contribution and extract the three elements above. Keep secondary outcomes out unless they are essential to that contribution.
2. Choose a structure, then place the central contribution early: the variable, the outcome, or the method, depending on what readers need to recognize first.
3. Add the comparator, design, and essential scope qualifiers. Preserve distinctions such as `female mice`, `external validation`, or `under drought conditions` when they limit the claim. Do not broaden a tissue result to a whole organism, a benchmark result to all applications, or a local sample to a global population.
4. Remove empty openings such as `A study of` or `An investigation into` when no meaning is lost. Replace a generic `evaluation` with the actual outcome rather than simply deleting it. Retain familiar technical search terms; remove unnecessary abbreviations, promotional novelty claims, and wordplay that obscures the subject.
5. Check the complete title against the source: variable, outcome, sample, direction, comparator, design, and claim strength must agree. Check grammar after compression and count against any explicit word or character limit. Do not impose a universal title-length rule.

Offer the requested number, or one recommendation with a small set of genuinely different alternatives when useful. Explain the difference in emphasis or claim strength briefly unless the user requests titles only. Do not generate many superficial synonym variants or include the extraction table in every answer.

### Worked title construction

The examples below are fictional editing exercises; their study facts are stipulated solely to demonstrate title construction.

**Experimental example:** Supplied facts establish that coating A reduces corrosion of steel during salt-spray testing.

- Elements: coating A → corrosion → steel under salt-spray testing.
- Incomplete title: `Evaluation of coating A on steel`.
- Finding-led title: `Coating A reduces corrosion of steel during salt-spray testing`.
- Descriptive alternative: `Corrosion resistance of steel with coating A during salt-spray testing`.

The revised titles name the measured property and preserve the test conditions. They do not claim improved field durability, which was not tested.

**Observational example:** Supplied facts establish an association between shift work and sleep quality in nurses in a cross-sectional study; the direction is not supplied.

- Elements: shift work → sleep quality → nurses; design: cross-sectional.
- Outcome/relationship-led title: `Association between shift work and sleep quality in nurses: a cross-sectional study`.
- Declarative alternative: `Shift work is associated with sleep quality in nurses: a cross-sectional study`.

Neither title invents an adverse direction or changes the association to `Shift work worsens sleep quality`. Select the formulation that best fits the intended emphasis and journal style.

## Keywords

### Generate a candidate pool

Start from what an intended reader would search for, using the supplied title, abstract, research question, and central findings. Extract concepts before choosing their wording:

| Concept source | Candidate content | Selection boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Research problem | Disease, phenomenon, process, or technical task | Prefer the specific problem over a generic field label. |
| Variable or intervention | Exposure, treatment, material, strategy, or algorithm | Use the actual entity; do not infer an unreported drug class or mechanism. |
| Main outcome | Endpoint, measured property, performance criterion, or relationship | Name what was assessed, not a promotional conclusion. |
| Evidence source | Population, tissue, organism, dataset type, or application domain | Include it when it helps distinguish the work; a broad organism name alone may contribute little. |
| Central mechanism or method | Tested pathway, defining assay, analytical method, or study design | Include only if supported and central. A routine assay is not automatically a useful keyword, but a method is central in a methods paper. |

Expand the pool with established synonyms, full names and familiar abbreviations, relevant controlled-vocabulary candidates, and terminology in supplied related papers. Do not import those papers' study facts or invent references. If external lookup is requested, verify unfamiliar vocabulary and inspect relevant papers or the appropriate subject thesaurus.

For a substantial manuscript, about 15 initial candidates can help avoid committing to the first few obvious words; shorter material may justify fewer. Favor meaningful phrases, often two or three words, when they resolve ambiguity. Keep an established one-word term or a longer technical phrase when it is the accurate search concept. These are brainstorming aids, not required counts or phrase lengths.

### Refine wording and remove weak candidates

Group candidates by concept before narrowing the list. Prefer a set that covers distinct important aspects of the study over several near-synonyms for the same aspect.

| Candidate problem | Action | Example or qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Too broad to identify the work | Add the defining concept when supported | `corrosion` may become `steel corrosion` when steel is the material actually studied. |
| Generic research label | Replace with the actual scientific subject | `biological factor`, `research`, or `evaluation` alone rarely identifies the question. |
| Administrative or publication metadata | Exclude from the scientific keyword list | Funding status does not describe the study's scientific content. |
| Routine technique with little relevance to the main contribution | Drop or deprioritize | Retain a technique when it is the research contribution or a defining search route. |
| Duplicate concept in several forms | Select the most useful form | Do not spend separate keyword slots on a full name and its abbreviation unless required or justified. |
| Unsupported mechanism, application, or benefit | Exclude | A correlation study does not justify a treatment-mechanism keyword. |
| Legitimately narrow concept with few publications | Retain if central and accurate | Low search volume alone is not a reason to discard a specialized term. |

Use recognizable terms rather than constructing an unusual phrase solely to reduce search-result counts. Call a term a verified controlled-vocabulary heading only after checking the relevant vocabulary; otherwise label it a candidate term.

### Optional database check

Ordinary keyword drafting can proceed from the supplied material. If the user asks for search-informed selection, use a relevant database and evaluate both the wording and the results:

1. Search each promising phrase and useful synonyms with a consistent field, date range, and matching approach. Account for database term mapping and phrase handling; counts from different query settings are not directly comparable.
2. Inspect the relevance of returned records, not just the hit count. A very broad set can suggest adding a qualifier; an unexpectedly empty set can suggest a spelling issue, unusual wording, or a genuinely specialized topic.
3. Prefer terms that accurately retrieve the intended topic. Do not choose a middle-sized count as an optimization target or discard a central term solely because it is common or rare.
4. When reporting verification, identify the database, query, relevant filters, and search date. Report counts only if actually retrieved. Distinguish search-informed choices from candidates selected without lookup.

### Assemble the submission list

Use the journal's supplied number and vocabulary requirements. Without a known limit, choose a concise set covering the central concepts; roughly 8–10 strong concepts can be a planning pool rather than a mandatory submission list. Coordinate the title, abstract, and keyword list: place the most important concepts naturally in the title and abstract, and use keyword slots to add useful coverage.

One planning arrangement is two central concepts in the title and six to eight additional concepts in the keyword list, when the manuscript and journal permit. Do not force that split, pad a short study to meet it, or prohibit overlap with the title when accuracy or journal requirements favor it. Do not repeat terms mechanically to create keyword density or claim ranking, citation, or AI-discoverability gains.

Return the requested number as a clean, semicolon-separated list unless another format is requested. Without external verification, label the list **Candidate keywords**. Add a brief rationale or unresolved terminology query only when useful; do not show the entire brainstorming pool by default. If the requested count cannot be met without padding or unsupported concepts, explain the shortfall.

### Worked keyword selection

Fictional example: a cross-sectional study examines the association between shift work and sleep quality in nurses. No biological mechanism or treatment is evaluated.

- Candidate concepts: shift work; sleep quality; nurses; cross-sectional study; work; health; melatonin; treatment.
- Keep the first four: they identify the exposure, outcome, population, and design.
- Remove `work` and `health` as unnecessarily broad here; exclude `melatonin` and `treatment` because the supplied study does not investigate them.
- **Candidate keywords:** Shift work; sleep quality; nurses; cross-sectional study.

This list is based on the supplied content, not a database search or a verified vocabulary mapping. It may overlap with the title; do not replace accurate central concepts with peripheral ones merely to avoid repetition.

## Abstracts

An abstract should let a reader identify:

| Reader need | Content |
|---|---|
| Reason for the work | Specific problem and supported research need |
| Approach | Aim, design, evidence source, and comparison |
| Findings | Principal results, effect magnitude, and relevant uncertainty |
| Meaning | Evidence-calibrated answer and essential scope limitations |

Apply these functions in a narrative abstract or distribute them under the supplied structured headings. Keep background brief, state the actual design and comparison, report the principal findings with appropriate uncertainty, and give a bounded answer. Follow known journal requirements; do not invent mandatory headings.

Optional drafting frames below describe functions, not fixed sentence requirements:

- Background/gap: `[Problem] remains unresolved because [supported limitation].`
- Aim/method: `We evaluated [question] using [design] in [population].`
- Result: `Compared with [comparator], [group] had [outcome estimate and uncertainty] at [time].`
- Conclusion: `These findings [supported answer], with [important limitation if needed].`

Use only slots supported by the source material. Do not fill missing numbers with plausible values. Prioritize primary results over extensive background and retain negative primary findings. Avoid vague endings such as `The implications are discussed`. Expand necessary abbreviations and make the abstract understandable without the main text or figures.

When only the abstract is available, assess internal consistency rather than claiming whole-paper agreement. If abstract and main text conflict, identify the exact discrepancy instead of choosing a number. Count the final version against any explicit limit and state whether headings count; do not claim compliance with an unknown submission-system counter.
